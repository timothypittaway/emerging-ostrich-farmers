# Emerging ostrich farmers and supply chain participation

Public materials from my doctoral thesis on how emerging (smallholder) farmers in the Eastern Cape, South Africa, can take part in the ostrich industry's export supply chain.

> Pittaway, T. (2013). *Exploring Interventions for Participation of Emerging Farmers in the Eastern Cape Ostrich-Industry Supply Chain*. Doctor Technologiae thesis, Nelson Mandela Metropolitan University, Port Elizabeth, South Africa. Promoter: Prof P. van Niekerk. Co-promoter: Dr J. Vansteenkiste.

## Why this study

South Africa's ostrich industry earns most of its value through export markets, especially the EU. Entry is demanding: biosecurity and registration protocols (VPN04), avian influenza controls, intensive husbandry, high feed costs and a complex chain of intermediaries.

At the time, no study had looked at how emerging farmers in the Eastern Cape could take part in this supply chain, or at what support they would need.

## Approach

An exploratory, mixed-methods design across five emerging farmer programmes in the Eastern Cape:

- literature and content analysis, including text-mining of ostrich research
- a situational analysis of the industry (micro and macro environment)
- comparative case studies, with a household questionnaire and focus groups
- qualitative coding in ATLAS.ti using grounded theory
- a new **participation capacity instrument** to compare programmes

## Main findings

- For several programmes, land access rather than ostrich farming was the main driver, which left limited capacity for intensive production.
- VPN04 biosecurity and record-keeping requirements were very costly for poor rural farmers.
- Programmes depended heavily on mentors, which weakened their own market engagement, price leverage and access to market information.
- Even so, programmes added value as land reform vehicles, sources of rural jobs and out-growers for commercial farms.

None of the programmes reached the capacity needed to participate fully, but the study argued that targeted interventions could move them towards commercial farming.

## What is not here

No participant data is shared. That includes questionnaire responses, farmer profiles, focus group material and programme-level scores. Participants took part on the basis of anonymity, and the programmes are small enough that individuals could be identified.

## Link to later work

The Local Vulnerability Index in this thesis was an early form of the idea I now develop as **transitional vulnerability**: how farms and rural businesses become exposed while they go through structural change.
